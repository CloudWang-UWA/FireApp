## 4. Risk and Technology Assessments

### 4.1 Risk Assessment

This project involves collecting and analysing spatial data for a selected region, including environmental factors such as vegetation, slope, elevation, fuel load, and wind conditions, in order to estimate the fire vulnerability of potential cultural heritage sites. The system focuses on identifying how different environmental conditions may influence the likelihood and severity of fire impact on these sites, rather than predicting fire occurrence itself.

In addition, the vulnerability of a site also depends on the type of heritage present, as different materials (e.g., stone, wood, or organic materials) respond differently to fire exposure. The model is designed to provide simple classifications (e.g., low, medium, high risk) to ensure usability and clarity for end users.

The project also involves handling culturally sensitive heritage-related information, which introduces ethical considerations around data access and representation. As a result, several technical, operational, and ethical risks have been identified and are considered early in development to ensure appropriate mitigation strategies are in place.

**Data Risks**  
The system relies on publicly available spatial datasets, including environmental data such as vegetation, slope, elevation, fuel load, and wind-related indicators, as well as indicators of potential cultural heritage locations. These datasets may be incomplete, inconsistent, or vary in resolution depending on the source. In some cases, relevant data may also be limited or unavailable for specific regions. The relevance of selected areas may also depend on environmental characteristics (e.g., presence of granite outcrops or other geological features), and not all regions may be suitable for meaningful analysis.

**Mitigation:**  
Datasets will be reviewed and validated before use, and only reliable and relevant data sources will be selected. Where data is missing, simplified assumptions may be used where appropriate. Preprocessing steps will be applied to standardise formats and remove inconsistencies to ensure the data is suitable for analysis.

**Data Integration and GIS Risks**  
The project requires combining multiple spatial datasets from different sources, which may use different coordinate systems, formats, or levels of detail. This can lead to issues such as misaligned layers, incorrect positioning of features, or data loss during conversion (e.g., when transforming data into GeoJSON for use in the application). These issues can affect the accuracy of both the analysis and the final output.

**Mitigation:**  
All datasets will be converted to a consistent coordinate reference system before integration. Intermediate results will be checked during preprocessing to ensure correct alignment, and data transformations will be tested to minimise loss of accuracy.

**Risk Model and Algorithm Risks**  
The fire vulnerability assessment in this project is based on a simplified, rule-based approach that uses environmental factors such as vegetation, slope, elevation, fuel load, and wind conditions, along with the type of heritage present. Since the model does not simulate real wildfire behaviour, it may not fully capture the complexity of how fires spread under different conditions. Incorrect assumptions or weighting of these factors may also lead to misleading risk classifications.

**Mitigation:**  
The model will remain transparent and based on clearly defined rules, with all assumptions documented. The focus will be on providing an indicative assessment rather than precise prediction, and the model can be refined based on feedback and testing during development.

**Ethical and Cultural Data Risks**  
The project involves cultural heritage information, which may be sensitive, particularly in relation to the location and significance of sites. Exposing or misrepresenting such data may conflict with Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) principles and Indigenous Data Sovereignty (IDaS) requirements. There is also a risk that simplified representations of risk may not fully reflect the cultural importance of certain sites.

**Mitigation:**  
Sensitive location data will be handled carefully and not unnecessarily exposed within the system. Where appropriate, data may be generalised or restricted to prevent misuse. The design of the system will aim to respect cultural considerations and align with guidance provided by the client.

**Performance Risks**  
The application involves processing and analysing geospatial datasets, which may vary in size and complexity depending on the selected region and data sources. Handling larger datasets may lead to slower processing times, increased memory usage, or delays in generating results, particularly during data preprocessing and risk calculation. In addition, the system may be used in regional or remote areas where network connectivity is limited or unstable, which could further affect data loading and system responsiveness if the application relies on online resources.

**Mitigation:**  
The MVP will focus on a limited geographic region to reduce data size and complexity. Data will be filtered and simplified where necessary, and processing steps will be kept efficient to ensure acceptable performance. Where possible, the system will minimise reliance on continuous network connectivity by using preprocessed or locally available data. The system will also be developed incrementally to monitor performance and identify bottlenecks early.

**Project Timeline Risks**  
The project involves multiple stages, including data collection, preprocessing, risk model development, and system implementation, all of which must be completed within a limited timeframe, with the final system due by the end of May. This relatively short timeline increases the risk of delays, particularly in sourcing and preparing datasets or resolving unexpected technical challenges during development.

**Mitigation:**  
The project will follow an iterative Agile approach, allowing tasks to be broken down into smaller, manageable components. Regular progress tracking and milestone reviews will be used to identify delays early, and priority will be given to delivering core MVP features first to ensure the system is completed within the available timeframe.

**Team Capability Risks**  
While the team has a strong overall foundation in web development, data processing, and software engineering, not all members have extensive experience with GIS-specific workflows or wildfire-related analysis. This may introduce a learning curve during the early stages of the project, particularly when working with spatial data and integrating it into the application.

**Mitigation:**  
Tasks will be allocated based on individual strengths, with members experienced in web development, GIS, and data analysis taking lead roles in relevant areas. Knowledge gaps will be addressed through collaborative learning, documentation, and incremental development to ensure steady progress.

### 4.2 Skills and Resources Analysis

The team has a balanced combination of skills that aligns well with the technical requirements of the project. Two members have strong experience in web development, particularly in building frontend interfaces and integrating backend services, which supports the development of the web-based system. One member has completed a GIS-related unit at UWA, providing relevant knowledge in handling spatial data, mapping concepts, and basic spatial analysis. In addition, one member has experience in scripting and data analytics, which is useful for preprocessing datasets and implementing the rule-based fire risk assessment.

More broadly, all team members are familiar with web development fundamentals, and most have experience working across both frontend and backend components, as well as with databases and basic spatial data processing. This shared technical foundation reduces reliance on any single individual and allows tasks to be distributed effectively across the team.

The team also has access to standard development and collaboration tools such as GitHub, Microsoft Teams, and OneDrive, which are already being used for version control, communication, and documentation. These tools support coordination, task tracking, and iterative development throughout the project.

The project also relies on publicly available environmental and spatial datasets, which are accessible but may vary in quality and completeness. These constraints are manageable within the scope of the MVP.

In terms of workload, team members are able to contribute consistently throughout the semester, although this must be balanced with other academic commitments. Given the limited timeframe, the project scope has been kept focused on a clearly defined MVP to ensure it remains achievable.

While some aspects of the project, particularly GIS data handling and risk modelling, may be less familiar to certain team members, these gaps will be addressed through incremental learning, documentation, and collaboration during development.

Overall, the combination of web development skills, GIS knowledge, and data processing capability makes the project feasible within the available timeframe, provided development remains focused on the agreed MVP scope and avoids unnecessary feature expansion.

### 4.3 Technology Choices and Justification

At this stage, the system is planned as a web-based application that separates data processing from the user interface. This approach allows flexibility in how data is handled and presented, while keeping the system modular and easier to maintain as the project evolves.

**Frontend (React – JavaScript/TypeScript)**  
The frontend is proposed to be developed using React, which is well suited for building interactive and responsive user interfaces. Since the system may involve displaying results, filtering data, or interacting with processed outputs, React provides a flexible structure for handling dynamic content. It also supports component-based development, making it easier to manage and extend the interface over time. Several team members have a strong foundation in React and general web development, which supports efficient implementation. TypeScript may be used to improve code maintainability and reduce runtime errors, although JavaScript remains a viable option during early development.

**Backend (Flask – Python)**  
The backend is proposed to be implemented using Flask, a lightweight Python framework that is suitable for building simple and flexible APIs. Python is particularly appropriate for this project as it supports data processing and scripting, which are required for handling datasets and implementing the rule-based fire risk calculation. Members of the team also have experience working with Python, which supports the use of Flask for backend development. Flask allows the backend to remain minimal while still supporting core functionalities such as data processing, risk calculation, and serving results to the frontend.

**Data Processing and Format Handling**  
The system will rely on processing spatial datasets and converting them into formats suitable for analysis and use within the application. Python-based workflows are well suited for this purpose, particularly for transforming raw data into structured formats such as CSV or GeoJSON. These formats also support project requirements such as exporting results for further use or reporting.

**Architecture Justification**  
The proposed separation between frontend and backend ensures that data processing and user interaction are handled independently. This makes the system easier to debug, test, and extend. It also allows the project to adapt if requirements change, for example if the system evolves from a simple data-processing tool into a more interactive application. This approach aligns with the goal of providing a simple and accessible tool for land managers rather than a complex simulation system.

**Alternative Approaches**  
Alternative approaches such as a standalone desktop application or a single monolithic system were considered. However, a web-based approach is preferred due to its flexibility, ease of access, and suitability for demonstration and future extension. At the same time, the design remains flexible enough to support simpler implementations if required, such as processing input data (e.g., CSV or GeoJSON) and returning calculated risk outputs without a complex interface.

Overall, the proposed technology stack is appropriate for the project as it supports data processing, flexibility in implementation, and incremental development, while remaining consistent with the team’s existing skills and the scope of the MVP.
