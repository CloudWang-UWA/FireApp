### **Agenda**
- Clarify the project goal and expected outcomes
- Discuss factors influencing fire vulnerability of heritage sites
- Discuss potential modelling approaches using GIS
- Identify possible datasets and tools for the project
- Discuss possible study areas for the initial assessment

### **Discussion**
- Sean explained that the main goal of the project is to identify cultural heritage sites that may be at risk from bushfires. The system should help support mitigation activities before wildfires occur.

- Some heritage sites are particularly fragile and vulnerable to fire damage. Examples include scar trees, organic cultural materials, and certain types of stone that can be damaged by extreme heat and radiant heat from fires. **Granite outcrops** were highlighted as especially vulnerable locations.

- The project should aim to develop a **predictive GIS model** that can estimate where unmapped heritage sites may exist based on landscape characteristics. Geological and landscape features, such as granite formations, can help identify areas where heritage sites are more likely to be located.

- Aerial photography and geological datasets from sources such as **Data WA** may be used to identify geological formations and landscape features that are associated with heritage sites.

- The main users of the system are expected to be people involved in **prescribed burning and firefighting operations**, particularly staff working with DBCA.

- Several **environmental factors** influencing fire behaviour were discussed. These include slope, vegetation fuel load, and proximity to water sources. Fire tends to burn more intensely when moving uphill. It was noted that for every 10 degrees of slope, the fire can spread approximately twice as fast compared to burning downhill.

- Vegetation and fuel load are also important factors. Areas with dense or older vegetation may result in more intense fires. Heritage sites located near vegetation that can burn may therefore be at higher risk.

- One possible modelling approach suggested by Sean is to create a **risk classification system** based on factors such as slope and distance from vegetation. For example, sites located on flat terrain may be considered low risk, while sites located on steeper slopes and closer to vegetation may be assigned higher risk levels.

- Sean recommended selecting an initial **study area** from the DBCA **Burn Options Program**, which identifies areas where prescribed burns are likely to occur. The team could then assess the fire risk for heritage sites within one selected area.

- Several candidate burn areas were mentioned, including FRK-114, FRK-113, FRK-115, FRK-116, FRK-108, FRK-094, ALB-042, ALB-071, and ALB-081. These areas are located around regions such as Franklin, Albany, and the Stirling Ranges.

- Possible datasets and tools discussed include **DBCA Fuel Age datasets**, vegetation data from **Data WA**, and GIS software such as **QGIS** for spatial analysis and modelling.

### **Agreed Task**
- The team will review the suggested literature and datasets related to fire behaviour and vegetation.
- The team will explore available datasets from Data WA and DBCA, including vegetation and fuel age data.
- The team will investigate potential study areas from the DBCA Burn Options Program and select a suitable region containing granite formations.
- The team will begin exploring how slope, vegetation proximity, and geological features can be incorporated into a simple fire vulnerability model using GIS tools.
